Why Does AI Keep Saying “It’s Not X, It’s Y”?

You’ve seen it. You ask an AI a straightforward question and get back: “It’s not just a tool — it’s a paradigm shift.” You ask it to help you work faster and it pivots to: “It’s not about efficiency; it’s about transformation.” You send a two-line question and get back something that reads like a Series A pitch.

The “It’s not X, it’s Y” construction has become the most recognizable fingerprint of AI-generated text. Once you notice it, you cannot stop noticing it. And the more you use AI chatbots and writing tools, the harder it becomes to ignore.

This post explains where the pattern comes from at an architectural level, and gives you prompt engineering strategies that actually suppress it.

The Pattern Has a Name

In classical rhetoric, this construction is called antithesis, or negative-positive parallelism, or contrast framing. It works by first negating a familiar assumption, then replacing it with something supposedly more expansive or profound.

When used by human writers, it’s a high-impact device deployed sparingly. Advertising copywriters use it to reframe value propositions. Political speechwriters use it to signal a turning point. The entire effect depends on restraint.

A language model has no concept of restraint, applying the structure indiscriminately whether describing a recipe, explaining a bug fix, or summarizing a business report. A rhetorical technique designed for occasional emphasis becomes a verbal tic repeated across every response :facepalm:

Where the Pattern Comes From

LLMs are trained on vast, largely uncurated slices of the internet. The internet is disproportionately populated by text written to sell, persuade, or capture attention: content marketing, SEO articles, LinkedIn thought-leadership posts, corporate press releases. These formats rely heavily on contrast framing. Brands write things like “We don’t do ego here, we do excellence” and “Where you’re poured into, not just managed.”

During pre-training, the model has no mechanism to separate the factual content from the rhetorical packaging. It learns the statistical distribution of “authoritative-sounding text,” and that distribution is saturated with antithesis structures. Research into persuasion and bias in LLMs confirms that models absorb the persuasive frameworks of their training data alongside the facts, with no mechanism to distinguish between the two.

The model encodes “It’s not X, it’s Y” as a statistical marker of expertise, because that is precisely how it appears across millions of training examples.

How RLHF Locks It In

Pre-training loads the vocabulary of marketing copy into the model, and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) locks it in as a behavioral default.

RLHF is the fine-tuning stage where human annotators compare model responses and rank the better one. Those rankings train a Reward Model, which then scores and optimizes future outputs at scale. Annotators follow “helpfulness” rubrics that reward nuance, multi-faceted perspectives, and comprehensive insights.

The problem is that annotators under time pressure routinely conflate the appearance of nuance with actual depth. A response that says “Photosynthesis isn’t merely a biological function — it’s the foundational engine of life on Earth” consistently outranks one that says “Photosynthesis converts light into energy,” even though the second is more precise. Over millions of training iterations, the Reward Model learns a proxy objective: rhetorical elaboration earns higher scores than declarative accuracy.

RLHF also has a safety dimension. Models are penalized heavily for being abrasive or overly definitive. The “It’s not X, it’s Y” structure lets the model validate the user’s starting assumption before pivoting to a safer or broader claim, which is a face-saving maneuver rooted in politeness theory. The model gets rewarded for generating frictionless output, and the antithesis structure is the most frictionless rhetorical shape available.

The Math Behind It

Recent research has shown that both RLHF and the newer Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) are mathematically equivalent to contrastive learning: a paradigm where a model is trained by pulling toward positive examples and pushing away from negative ones. In RLHF terms, the “chosen” response is the positive sample and the “rejected” response is the negative one.

As a consequence, the model learns to structurally oppose its rejected samples within its own output. When rejected responses tend to be brief and flatly declarative, and chosen responses tend to be verbose and expansive, the model starts generating text that explicitly names and distances itself from the simpler framing before committing to the preferred one. “It’s not X” is the model pushing away from the negative sample space, and “It’s Y” is the model pulling toward the positive one.

This is why telling the model to stop using a specific phrase rarely works. The underlying optimization pressure remains, and the model finds a synonymous structure: “While X is true, Y is paramount” replaces the banned phrasing while preserving the same dialectical shape.

Prompt Engineering Strategies That Actually Work

Simple phrase bans have low effectiveness. The model substitutes synonymous structures because the reward signal driving the behavior is embedded in the model’s architecture, beyond the reach of vocabulary-level instructions.

The approach most likely to help is to this as a single block to your system prompt or custom instructions:

“Prohibit thesis-antithesis patterns, dialectical hedging, concessive frameworks, and rhetorical equivocation. State the main point directly and build from it linearly. Avoid unwarranted rhetorical balance. Do not define something by stating what it is not. Direct, declarative claims are preferred over hedging with concessive framing. Treat contrastive structures as high-impact rhetorical tools, used at most once per major section.”

The reason to use academic and rhetorical terminology rather than plain instructions like “write naturally” is that vague instructions have no stable internal mapping in the model. Terms like “dialectical hedging” and “concessive framing” land closer to the model’s actual representational categories, which is why they suppress the behavior more reliably than asking it to sound human.

The Takeaway

The “It’s not X, it’s Y” pattern is a direct consequence of training AI to perform the appearance of helpfulness over actual precision. The marketing-heavy pre-training corpus established the statistical prior, RLHF’s preference for rhetorical elaboration over declarative accuracy hardwired it, and the contrastive mathematics of DPO made the pattern structurally inevitable.

Until alignment paradigms shift toward rewarding declarative accuracy over rhetorical elaboration, users who want direct output from their AI tools will need to actively engineer against the model’s defaults. The strategies above are a starting point. The core principle is to target structure and category, because the behavior lives at the architectural level, and that is exactly where your instructions need to land.
